1. Spontaneous symmetry breaking

A symmetry is spontaneously broken when the equations (the Lagrangian) are invariant under a transformation, but the lowest-energy state, the vacuum, is not. The laws keep the symmetry; the state that nature settles into does not.

The classic everyday example is a ferromagnet. The laws of electromagnetism don't single out any direction, yet below the Curie temperature the spins line up along some direction. Every direction would have worked equally well. The magnet had to pick one.

Goldstone's 1961 insight was about what happens next. If the broken symmetry is continuous (like rotations, not like a left/right flip), there are infinitely many equivalent vacua connected to each other. Moving between them costs no energy. In a relativistic quantum field theory, "an excitation that costs zero energy at zero momentum" means a massless particle.

2. The Goldstone model, computed

Take one complex scalar field \(\phi(x)\) with Lagrangian density

\[ \mathcal{L} = \partial_\mu \phi^*\,\partial^\mu \phi \;-\; V(\phi), \qquad V(\phi) = -\mu^2 |\phi|^2 + \lambda |\phi|^4, \qquad \mu^2>0,\ \lambda>0 . \]

The symmetry. \(\mathcal{L}\) depends only on \(|\phi|\) and \(|\partial\phi|\), so it is invariant under the global phase rotation

\[ \phi \;\to\; e^{i\alpha}\phi, \qquad \alpha \in \mathbb{R}. \]

This is the group \(U(1)\): rotations of a circle. It is continuous.

Finding the vacuum. Minimise \(V\) in \(|\phi|\):

\[ \frac{\partial V}{\partial |\phi|} = -2\mu^2|\phi| + 4\lambda|\phi|^3 = 0 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; |\phi|^2 = \frac{\mu^2}{2\lambda} \equiv \frac{v^2}{2}, \qquad v = \frac{\mu}{\sqrt{\lambda}} . \]

Because \(\mu^2>0\), the point \(\phi=0\) is a local maximum. The minima form a whole circle, \(|\phi| = v/\sqrt2\), in the complex \(\phi\)-plane. Completing the square makes the shape obvious:

\[ V = \lambda\left(|\phi|^2 - \tfrac{v^2}{2}\right)^2 - \frac{\lambda v^4}{4}. \]

Expanding around one vacuum. Choose the vacuum on the real axis, \(\langle\phi\rangle = v/\sqrt2\), and write fluctuations in Cartesian form:

\[ \phi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(v + h(x) + i\,\pi(x)\big), \]

with \(h,\pi\) real. Then \(|\phi|^2 - v^2/2 = \tfrac12\big(2vh + h^2 + \pi^2\big)\), so

\[ V = \frac{\lambda}{4}\big(2vh + h^2 + \pi^2\big)^2 + \text{const} = \underbrace{\lambda v^2\, h^2}_{\text{mass term}} \;+\; \lambda v\, h\,(h^2+\pi^2) \;+\; \frac{\lambda}{4}(h^2+\pi^2)^2 + \text{const}. \]

The kinetic term is \(\tfrac12(\partial h)^2 + \tfrac12(\partial\pi)^2\). Comparing to the standard form \(\tfrac12 m^2 h^2\):

\[ \boxed{\,m_h^2 = 2\lambda v^2 = 2\mu^2, \qquad m_\pi^2 = 0\,} \]

There is no \(\pi^2\) term at all. \(\pi\) is the Goldstone boson: massless and spin-0. The interaction terms (\(h\pi^2\), \(\pi^4\), …) don't spoil this. In polar variables \(\phi = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(v+\rho)\,e^{i\theta/v}\), the field \(\theta\) appears in \(\mathcal L\) only with derivatives, since a constant shift of \(\theta\) is just the original \(U(1)\) symmetry. Without derivatives there can be no mass term for \(\theta\) at any order.

3. The Goldstone theorem (proof sketch)

Goldstone conjectured the general result in 1961. Goldstone, Salam and Weinberg proved it in 1962:

Goldstone theorem In a Lorentz-invariant field theory, if a continuous global symmetry of the Lagrangian is not a symmetry of the vacuum, the spectrum contains a massless spin-0 particle for each broken symmetry generator.

Ingredient 1: Noether's theorem. A continuous symmetry gives a conserved current and charge:

\[ \partial_\mu j^\mu = 0, \qquad Q = \int d^3x\; j^0(\mathbf{x},t), \qquad [Q, H] = 0 . \]

For the model above, \(j^\mu = i(\phi^*\partial^\mu\phi - \phi\,\partial^\mu\phi^*)\).

Ingredient 2: broken means \(Q|0\rangle \neq 0\). Spontaneous breaking means some field \(\varphi\) has \(\langle 0|\delta\varphi|0\rangle \neq 0\), where \(\delta\varphi = i[Q,\varphi]\) is the symmetry variation. In our model \(\delta\pi \propto v\), so \(\langle 0|[Q,\pi]|0\rangle \propto v \neq 0\). That is only possible if \(Q\) does not annihilate the vacuum.

Ingredient 3: zero-energy states. Since \([Q,H]=0\), the state \(Q|0\rangle\) has the same energy as the vacuum. Strictly, \(Q|0\rangle\) is not normalisable in infinite volume, so we smear the charge density with a long-wavelength profile:

\[ Q_{\mathbf k} = \int d^3x\; e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\, j^0(\mathbf{x}) . \]

Current conservation gives \(\dot Q_{\mathbf k} = i\,\mathbf{k}\cdot\!\int e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}\,\mathbf{j}\), which vanishes as \(\mathbf k\to 0\). So \(Q_{\mathbf k}|0\rangle\) is a state of momentum \(\mathbf k\) whose energy goes to zero as \(\mathbf k\to0\). Lorentz invariance gives \(E^2 = \mathbf k^2 + m^2\), so \(E\to0\) at \(\mathbf k\to0\) forces \(m=0\).

The careful version uses the spectral (Källén–Lehmann) decomposition of \(\langle 0|[j^\mu(x),\varphi(0)]|0\rangle\). Current conservation forces its spectral weight to sit entirely at \(p^2=0\). Since the weight is nonzero when the symmetry is broken, a massless one-particle pole must exist.

The theorem's escape clauses The proof relies on (a) a global symmetry, (b) Lorentz invariance (or at least locality and translation invariance), and (c) an exact symmetry. Each loophole matters physically: (a) a local gauge symmetry lets the Goldstone be "eaten" (the Higgs mechanism, §6); (b) without Lorentz invariance, the number and dispersion of Goldstones can change (magnons); (c) a small explicit breaking gives a small mass, a "pseudo-Goldstone" (pions).

4. Counting Goldstone bosons

Let the Lagrangian have symmetry group \(G\) and the vacuum keep only a subgroup \(H\subset G\). Each broken generator is an independent flat direction in the space of vacua \(G/H\):

\[ N_{\text{Goldstone}} = \dim G - \dim H \qquad \text{(relativistic case)}. \]
SystemBreaking patterndim G − dim HGoldstones
Complex scalar (above)\(U(1)\to\{1\}\)1 − 0 = 1\(\pi\)
\(N\)-component real scalar\(O(N)\to O(N-1)\)\(\tfrac{N(N-1)}2 - \tfrac{(N-1)(N-2)}2 = N-1\)\(N-1\)
Two-flavour QCD\(SU(2)_L\times SU(2)_R \to SU(2)_V\)6 − 3 = 3\(\pi^+,\pi^-,\pi^0\)
Antiferromagnet\(SO(3)\to SO(2)\)3 − 1 = 22 linear magnons
Ferromagnet\(SO(3)\to SO(2)\)3 − 1 = 21 quadratic magnon

The ferromagnet is the exception. Without Lorentz invariance, two broken generators \(Q_a, Q_b\) can pair into a single "type-B" mode if \(\langle 0|[Q_a,Q_b]|0\rangle \neq 0\). In a ferromagnet, \([S_x,S_y]=iS_z\) and \(\langle S_z\rangle\) is the magnetisation, which is nonzero. The general rule (Watanabe–Murayama, Hidaka, 2012):

\[ n_A + 2n_B = \dim G - \dim H, \qquad n_B = \tfrac12\,\mathrm{rank}\,\rho_{ab},\quad \rho_{ab} = \lim_{V\to\infty}\tfrac{-i}{V}\langle 0|[Q_a,Q_b]|0\rangle . \]

5. Where they show up in nature

Pions: pseudo-Goldstone bosons

With massless up and down quarks, QCD would have an exact \(SU(2)_L\times SU(2)_R\) symmetry, broken by the quark condensate \(\langle\bar q q\rangle\neq0\). The pions would then be exactly massless. Real quarks have small masses that break the symmetry slightly. The Gell-Mann–Oakes–Renner relation quantifies the result:

\[ m_\pi^2 f_\pi^2 = -(m_u+m_d)\,\langle \bar q q\rangle + O(m_q^2), \]

so \(m_\pi^2\propto m_q\). This is why pions (≈140 MeV) are much lighter than other hadrons (proton ≈ 938 MeV). They are "almost" Goldstone bosons.

Spin waves

For a Heisenberg ferromagnet \(H = -J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\mathbf S_i\cdot\mathbf S_j\), linearising around the ordered state gives magnons with \(\omega(\mathbf k) \approx 2JS\,a^2 k^2\) at small \(k\). This is the gapless, quadratic Goldstone mode of broken spin-rotation symmetry.

Superfluid phonons

In superfluid helium the order parameter is \(\langle\psi\rangle = \sqrt{n_s}\,e^{i\theta}\), which breaks \(U(1)\) particle-number symmetry. The phase \(\theta\) is the Goldstone field, and its quanta are the sound waves (phonons, \(\omega = c_s k\)). These are the "quantized vibrations in excited fluids" mentioned in the obituary.

6. What gauge fields change: the Higgs mechanism

Now make the \(U(1)\) local, so that \(\alpha=\alpha(x)\). Invariance then requires a gauge field \(A_\mu\) and a covariant derivative:

\[ \mathcal L = |D_\mu\phi|^2 - V(\phi) - \tfrac14 F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}, \qquad D_\mu = \partial_\mu - ieA_\mu . \]

Write \(\phi = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(v+\rho)e^{i\theta/v}\). A gauge transformation with \(\alpha = -\theta/v\) removes \(\theta\) completely ("unitary gauge"). What remains is

\[ |D_\mu\phi|^2 \;\supset\; \tfrac12 e^2 v^2 A_\mu A^\mu \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m_A = e v . \]
Counting degrees of freedom Before: a massless photon (2 polarisations) + complex scalar (2 real fields) = 4.
After: a massive vector (3 polarisations) + one real Higgs \(\rho\) = 4.
The Goldstone boson becomes the photon's longitudinal polarisation. This is the Englert–Brout–Higgs–Guralnik–Hagen–Kibble mechanism (1964). In the Standard Model, three would-be Goldstones of \(SU(2)\times U(1)\to U(1)_{\text{em}}\) become the longitudinal \(W^+,W^-,Z\).

7. Adiabatic quantum computation

Goldstone's later career moved to quantum algorithms. In 2000, Farhi, Goldstone, Gutmann and Sipser proposed encoding an optimisation problem as finding the ground state of a "problem Hamiltonian" \(H_P\) that is diagonal in the computational basis:

\[ H_P\,|z\rangle = C(z)\,|z\rangle, \qquad z\in\{-1,+1\}^n . \]

Start in the easy ground state of a "driver" \(H_B = -\sum_j X_j\), which is \(|+\rangle^{\otimes n}\), the uniform superposition over all \(2^n\) strings. Then slowly interpolate:

\[ H(s) = (1-s)\,H_B + s\,H_P, \qquad s = t/T \in[0,1]. \]

The adiabatic theorem says the state stays close to the instantaneous ground state if the run time satisfies

\[ T \;\gg\; \frac{\max_s \|\partial_s H\|}{g_{\min}^2}, \qquad g_{\min} = \min_s\big(E_1(s)-E_0(s)\big). \]

At \(s=1\), measuring gives the optimal \(z\). The catch is that for hard problems the gap \(g_{\min}\) can be exponentially small. The run time then becomes exponential.

8. QAOA: the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm

Farhi, Goldstone and Gutmann introduced QAOA in 2014. Instead of slowly interpolating, it alternates the two Hamiltonians \(p\) times, with angles chosen to work best:

\[ |\boldsymbol\gamma,\boldsymbol\beta\rangle = e^{-i\beta_p B}\,e^{-i\gamma_p C}\cdots e^{-i\beta_1 B}\,e^{-i\gamma_1 C}\;|+\rangle^{\otimes n}, \qquad B=\sum_{j=1}^n X_j . \]

The figure of merit is the expected cost

\[ F_p(\boldsymbol\gamma,\boldsymbol\beta) = \langle\boldsymbol\gamma,\boldsymbol\beta|\,C\,|\boldsymbol\gamma,\boldsymbol\beta\rangle, \qquad M_p = \max_{\boldsymbol\gamma,\boldsymbol\beta} F_p \;\;(\text{or min, for energies}). \]

Key properties

  • It can only get better with depth. Setting \(\gamma_{p+1}=\beta_{p+1}=0\) recovers depth \(p\), so \(M_{p+1}\ge M_p\).
  • It reaches the optimum as \(p\to\infty\). A Trotterised version of the adiabatic path is one allowed choice of angles. So \(\lim_{p\to\infty}M_p = \max_z C(z)\) for any fixed \(n\).
  • It is local at small depth. If \(C\) is a sum of terms on a graph, the operator \(U^\dagger C_{jk} U\) only involves qubits within distance \(p\) of edge \((j,k)\). This "light cone" makes exact analysis possible, and it is also what limits shallow QAOA.

Example: the ring of disagrees

MaxCut on a ring of \(n\) vertices maximises \(C = \sum_{j}\tfrac12(1 - Z_jZ_{j+1})\). For even \(n\) the best cut is all \(n\) edges. FGG showed that depth-\(p\) QAOA reaches an approximation ratio of exactly \((2p+1)/(2p+2)\) for \(p=1,2\) and numerically beyond. They conjectured this holds for every \(p\). A June 2026 MIT paper reports a Lean-verified proof of this conjecture, found with Claude Fable 5.

9. The Sherrington–Kirkpatrick model and the Parisi value

The SK model is a spin glass in which every spin interacts with every other, with random couplings:

\[ C(\mathbf z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt n}\sum_{1\le jThe \(1/\sqrt n\) makes the ground-state energy scale like \(n\). Finding the ground state of a given instance is NP-hard in general. But the typical ground-state energy density, averaged over the randomness, is known exactly as \(n\to\infty\):

\[ \lim_{n\to\infty}\;\frac{1}{n}\,\min_{\mathbf z} C(\mathbf z) \;=\; -\,\Pi^* ,\qquad \Pi^* = 0.763166\ldots \]

\(\Pi^*\) is the Parisi value. Parisi computed it in 1979–80 using the replica trick,

\[ \mathbb E\ln Z = \lim_{m\to0}\frac{\mathbb E\,Z^m - 1}{m}, \qquad Z = \sum_{\mathbf z}e^{-C(\mathbf z)/T}. \]

He introduced replica symmetry breaking: the solution is described by a whole function \(q(x)\), \(x\in[0,1]\), that records how the "overlaps" between different low-energy states are distributed. The free energy is an infimum of a variational functional over such functions. Talagrand made this rigorous in 2006, and the zero-temperature (ground-state) version was later made rigorous as well.

Replica symmetry breaking is itself a kind of spontaneous symmetry breaking. The \(z\to -z\) symmetry and the symmetry between replicas are broken in a hierarchical, ultrametric way. This is not the same thing as Goldstone's continuous breaking, but the conceptual family resemblance is real.

QAOA on SK, at infinite size

In 2019, Farhi, Goldstone, Gutmann and Zhou derived a formula for the QAOA energy in the limit \(n\to\infty\), averaged over instances:

\[ V_p(\boldsymbol\gamma,\boldsymbol\beta) = \lim_{n\to\infty}\;\mathbb E_J\!\left[\frac{\langle\boldsymbol\gamma,\boldsymbol\beta|C|\boldsymbol\gamma,\boldsymbol\beta\rangle}{n}\right]. \]

The formula's cost grows like \(O(16^p)\). They also proved concentration: for large \(n\), a typical instance (and a typical measurement) gives energy close to \(V_p\). The optimal angles are therefore instance-independent. Later work (Basso, Farhi, Marwaha, Villalonga, Zhou, 2021) found a more efficient iteration and pushed the numerics to \(p=20\).

10. The conjecture and its proof

QAOA–Parisi conjecture (Basso–Farhi–Marwaha–Villalonga–Zhou, 2021) \[ \lim_{p\to\infty}\;\lim_{n\to\infty}\;\min_{\boldsymbol\gamma,\boldsymbol\beta} V_p(\boldsymbol\gamma,\boldsymbol\beta) \;=\; -\,\Pi^* . \]

Why the order of limits matters. For fixed \(n\), \(p\to\infty\) trivially reaches the optimum (§8). The content of the conjecture is that you first take \(n\to\infty\) at fixed depth, and only then let \(p\) grow. In that order, QAOA is a "local" algorithm at every finite stage. Local algorithms can be blocked by the overlap gap property (OGP), in which near-optimal solutions form well-separated clusters. QAOA has been proven to fall short of the Parisi value for some related problems where OGP holds (for example even \(q\ge4\) spin glasses). The SK model (\(q=2\)) is believed not to have OGP. That is why reaching \(\Pi^*\) was plausible there, but unproven.

Why it matters. If true, it means:

  1. QAOA is asymptotically optimal on typical SK instances. It finds near-ground states, not just good-but-suboptimal ones.
  2. The QAOA iteration gives a brand-new way to compute the Parisi constant, which doesn't need replicas.
  3. QAOA matches the best known classical result (Montanari's message-passing algorithm, which reaches \((1-\varepsilon)\Pi^*\) assuming no OGP). Matching is not the same as beating, so this is not a quantum speedup claim.

The proof. In the obituary, Edward Farhi says the conjecture was proven by an AI in what he calls "the OpenAI math dump." That most likely refers to OpenAI's October 6, 2026 release of 722 AI-generated manuscripts with Lean formalizations. I could not find independent coverage of the QAOA–Parisi result in that release specifically, so Farhi's statement is the main source for now. Expert review of that release is still ongoing.

The arc Goldstone's career began with the infinite-volume subtleties of symmetry breaking in field theory: why \(Q|0\rangle\) exists only as a limit, and why zero-momentum modes must be massless. It ended with the infinite-size limit of a quantum algorithm on a spin glass, where the order of limits \(n\to\infty\) and \(p\to\infty\) is the whole question. Both problems turn on what survives when a system becomes infinitely large.
