1. Summary

Yang–Mills theory is the mathematical framework behind the strong and weak nuclear forces. Physicists have used it for decades to make extremely accurate predictions, but nobody had ever proved that the quantum version exists as a rigorous mathematical object in our four-dimensional spacetime. The Clay Mathematics Institute's Millennium Prize problem asks for two things:

OpenAI says its model produced a natural-language existence proof on September 9, 2026. The company then formalised it in the Lean proof assistant (about 1,147 pages). A five-person panel chaired by Nobel laureate David Gross reviewed it and reported on November 10 that it proves existence of pure Yang–Mills theory "according to the problem statements which are standard in physics and mathematics." According to reports, the construction is built on two older human-developed tools: Wilson's lattice gauge theory (1974) and Tadeusz Balaban's renormalisation-group programme (1980s). That's probably what the Economist means by "the key insights appear to have come from human physicists."

2. Classical Yang–Mills theory

The ingredients

Fix a compact simple Lie group $G$, such as $SU(2)$ or $SU(3)$; quantum chromodynamics uses $SU(3)$. Let $\mathfrak g$ be its Lie algebra, with generators $T^a$. The basic field is a connection (gauge potential), a $\mathfrak g$-valued 1-form on spacetime:

$$A = A_\mu(x)\,dx^\mu = A^a_\mu(x)\,T^a\,dx^\mu .$$

Electromagnetism is the special case $G=U(1)$, where $A_\mu$ is the ordinary vector potential.

Covariant derivative and curvature

The covariant derivative and the field strength (curvature) are

$$D_\mu = \partial_\mu + A_\mu, \qquad F_{\mu\nu} = [D_\mu,D_\nu] = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu + [A_\mu, A_\nu].$$

In form language, $F = dA + A\wedge A$. The commutator term $[A_\mu,A_\nu]$ is the whole story: it vanishes for $U(1)$ (photons don't interact with each other) but not for non-abelian $G$ (gluons do interact with each other).

Gauge symmetry

For any map $U:\mathbb R^4\to G$, the transformation

$$A \;\mapsto\; U A U^{-1} + U\,d(U^{-1}), \qquad F \;\mapsto\; U F U^{-1}$$

leaves all physics unchanged. Physical quantities must therefore be gauge-invariant, such as $\operatorname{tr} F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}$ or the Wilson loop

$$W_C(A) = \operatorname{tr}\, \mathcal P \exp\!\Big(\oint_C A\Big),$$

the holonomy (parallel transport) around a closed curve $C$.

Action and field equations

In Euclidean signature (imaginary time, which is the setting used for rigorous constructions), the Yang–Mills action is

$$S_{\text{YM}}(A) = \frac{1}{4g^2}\int_{\mathbb R^4} \sum_{\mu,\nu} \big|F_{\mu\nu}(x)\big|^2\, d^4x = -\frac{1}{2g^2}\int \operatorname{tr}\,(F\wedge \star F),$$

where $g$ is the coupling constant. Minimising it gives the Yang–Mills equations $D^\mu F_{\mu\nu}=0$. These are nonlinear. Expanding $|F|^2$ produces cubic terms $\sim g\,\partial A\,A A$ and quartic terms $\sim g^2 A^4$, which are the gluon self-interactions.

3. Why quantising it is hard

Quantum field theory, in its Euclidean form, is formally defined by the "path integral"

$$\langle \mathcal O\rangle = \frac{1}{Z}\int \mathcal O(A)\, e^{-S_{\text{YM}}(A)}\,\mathcal DA, \qquad Z = \int e^{-S_{\text{YM}}(A)}\,\mathcal DA.$$

As written, this is not mathematics. Three separate problems arise:

  1. No Lebesgue measure on infinite-dimensional spaces. "$\mathcal DA$" does not exist. One must instead construct a probability measure on (generalised) gauge fields directly. Typical fields are so rough that they are distributions, not functions, so even $F^2$ is ill-defined pointwise.
  2. Gauge redundancy. Integrating over all $A$ counts each physical configuration infinitely often (once per gauge transformation). This must be handled on the quotient space $\mathcal A/\mathcal G$.
  3. Ultraviolet divergences. Perturbation theory gives infinite answers that must be renormalised. In 4D the coupling $g$ is dimensionless, which makes this the critical, hardest case.

Asymptotic freedom: what makes 4D Yang–Mills hopeful

Under renormalisation, the coupling "runs" with the energy scale $\mu$. To leading order the beta function is

$$\mu\frac{dg}{d\mu} = \beta(g) = -\frac{b_0}{16\pi^2}\,g^3 + O(g^5),\qquad b_0=\frac{11}{3}N \text{ for } SU(N).$$

The minus sign (Gross–Wilczek–Politzer, 1973; Nobel Prize 2004, which is the same David Gross who chaired the review panel) means that solving gives

$$g^2(\mu) \approx \frac{16\pi^2}{b_0\,\ln(\mu^2/\Lambda^2)} \;\xrightarrow{\;\mu\to\infty\;}\; 0.$$

4. The Clay problem: two parts

Clay statement (Jaffe–Witten, 2000), paraphrased Prove that for any compact simple gauge group $G$, a non-trivial quantum Yang–Mills theory exists on $\mathbb R^4$ and has a mass gap $\Delta>0$. "Exists" means satisfying axioms at least as strong as those of Wightman or Osterwalder–Schrader.

The mass gap, precisely

A quantum field theory comes with a Hilbert space $\mathcal H$, a vacuum vector $\Omega$, and a Hamiltonian $H\ge 0$ with $H\Omega=0$. The theory has a mass gap $\Delta>0$ if

$$\operatorname{spec}(H) \subset \{0\}\,\cup\,[\Delta,\infty).$$

Equivalently, in Euclidean language, connected correlations of gauge-invariant local observables decay exponentially:

$$\big|\langle \mathcal O(x)\,\mathcal O(0)\rangle - \langle \mathcal O\rangle^2\big| \;\le\; C\,e^{-\Delta |x|}.$$

5. What "exists" means: the Osterwalder–Schrader axioms

Existence means constructing a family of Euclidean correlation functions (Schwinger functions) $S_n(x_1,\dots,x_n)=\langle \mathcal O_1(x_1)\cdots\mathcal O_n(x_n)\rangle$ of gauge-invariant observables that satisfy:

AxiomContent
OS0 Regularity$S_n$ are tempered distributions with suitable growth bounds.
OS1 Euclidean invarianceInvariant under rotations and translations of $\mathbb R^4$: $S_n(Rx_i + a) = S_n(x_i)$.
OS2 Reflection positivityFor time reflection $\Theta$ and observables $F$ supported at positive time: $\langle \overline{\Theta F}\,F\rangle \ge 0$. This is the key property, because it produces a positive inner product.
OS3 Symmetry$S_n$ is symmetric under permuting its arguments.
OS4 ClusteringCorrelations factorise at large separation (a unique vacuum).
Osterwalder–Schrader reconstruction theorem (1973–75) If OS0–OS4 hold, there is a Hilbert space $\mathcal H$, a vacuum $\Omega$, a self-adjoint Hamiltonian $H\ge0$ and a unitary representation of the Poincaré group, giving a relativistic quantum theory satisfying the Wightman axioms. "Rotating back" from imaginary to real time is automatic.

"Non-trivial" adds the requirement that the theory not be Gaussian (free). Its correlations must show genuine interaction, for example nonzero connected 4-point functions.

6. Step 1: put it on a lattice (Wilson, 1974)

Replace $\mathbb R^4$ by a finite periodic lattice $\Lambda_{\varepsilon,L} = (\varepsilon\mathbb Z/L\mathbb Z)^4$ with spacing $\varepsilon$. Instead of the potential $A_\mu(x)$, put a group element on each link:

$$U_{x,\mu} \approx \exp\big(\varepsilon A_\mu(x)\big) \in G .$$

The parallel transport around a unit square (a plaquette) $p$ is

$$U_p = U_{x,\mu}\,U_{x+\varepsilon\hat\mu,\nu}\,U_{x+\varepsilon\hat\nu,\mu}^{-1}\,U_{x,\nu}^{-1} \;\approx\; \exp\big(\varepsilon^2 F_{\mu\nu}(x) + O(\varepsilon^3)\big).$$

The Wilson action and the lattice measure are

$$S_\varepsilon(U) = \frac{1}{g^2}\sum_{p}\operatorname{Re}\operatorname{tr}\big(\mathbf 1 - U_p\big),\qquad d\mu_\varepsilon(U) = \frac{1}{Z_\varepsilon}\,e^{-S_\varepsilon(U)}\prod_{\text{links}} dU_{x,\mu},$$

where $dU$ is normalised Haar measure on $G$. This is a perfectly well-defined probability measure on a finite product of compact groups. Using $U_p\approx e^{\varepsilon^2F}$ and $\operatorname{Re}\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf 1-e^{X})\approx -\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}X^2$, one checks that $S_\varepsilon \to S_{\text{YM}}$ formally as $\varepsilon\to0$. The entire difficulty is making that limit rigorous for the measures, not just the actions.

7. Step 2: Balaban's renormalisation group

As $\varepsilon\to0$ the number of variables explodes and naive estimates blow up. The solution, following Wilson's renormalisation-group (RG) idea made rigorous by Balaban, is to integrate out fluctuations one length scale at a time.

The RG map

Given the action $S_k$ at scale $L^k\varepsilon$, define a coarse "block" field $V = \mathcal B(U)$ by averaging over blocks of side $L$, and set

$$e^{-S_{k+1}(V)} = \int \delta\big(V - \mathcal B(U)\big)\,e^{-S_k(U)}\,dU .$$

The aim is to show that each $S_k$ keeps the form "Wilson action with a renormalised coupling $g_k$, plus small, local corrections", with $g_k$ following the asymptotic-freedom flow $g_{k+1}^{-2}\approx g_k^{-2} + \tfrac{b_0}{8\pi^2}\ln L$.

Balaban's key technical ideas (1982–1989)

  • Small-field / large-field split. Where the field is close to a pure gauge (curvature small), expand perturbatively around it. Where it is large, use the fact that such regions are exponentially suppressed by $e^{-S}$ with $g$ small.
  • Gauge fixing within blocks (axial-type gauges adapted to the block structure), avoiding the global Gribov ambiguities of gauge fixing.
  • Cluster / polymer expansions. These write the corrections as sums of local pieces with exponential decay, so that errors don't accumulate across the volume.
  • Main theorem (ultraviolet stability). On a finite torus, the partition function satisfies bounds uniform in the lattice spacing: $$e^{-C|\Lambda|}\;\le\;\frac{Z_\varepsilon}{Z_\varepsilon^{\text{free-ish}}}\;\le\;e^{C|\Lambda|}.$$
The gap Balaban left Balaban controlled the partition function (vacuum energy) in a finite box. He did not complete the construction of gauge-invariant correlation functions, their continuum limit, the infinite-volume limit, or verification of all the OS axioms. For roughly 35 years this was a famous unfinished programme. Reports describe the AI proof as building on exactly this framework and carrying it through.

8. Step 3: the continuum and infinite-volume limits

Compactness argument in a sentence

If the Schwinger functions $S_n^{(\varepsilon,L)}$ obey bounds uniform in $\varepsilon$ and $L$, then by a Prokhorov / Arzelà–Ascoli-type compactness theorem some subsequence $\varepsilon_j\to0,\ L_j\to\infty$ converges to limits $S_n$. Properties that are preserved under limits (reflection positivity, symmetry, translation invariance) pass to $S_n$ automatically. Others, notably full rotation invariance (the lattice only has 90° rotations) and non-triviality, need separate proofs. Different subsequences might in principle give different limits, which is why "uniqueness of the limit" is a separate, stronger statement.

9. Why the mass gap is much harder

Existence is fundamentally an ultraviolet problem: control short distances, where asymptotic freedom makes the coupling small. The mass gap is an infrared problem: control long distances, where the coupling becomes large and no small parameter exists.

  • Run the RG flow of Figure 5 toward large scales and $g_k$ grows. Balaban-style expansions need $g_k$ small, so they stop working near scale $\Lambda^{-1}$.
  • At very strong lattice coupling ($g$ large), a mass gap is provable by convergent strong-coupling expansions (Osterwalder–Seiler). The open problem is to connect the weak-coupling regime (needed for the continuum) to the strong-coupling regime (where the gap is visible), with no phase transition in between.
  • Closely related is confinement, the area law for large Wilson loops: $$\langle W_C\rangle \sim e^{-\sigma\,\mathrm{Area}(C)},\qquad \sigma>0,$$ which says quarks are bound by a constant force ("string tension"). It is widely expected but unproved.

The review committee's position is explicit: the construction supplies no evidence for or against a mass gap. Lattice computer simulations strongly suggest $\Delta>0$ (the lightest $SU(3)$ glueball has mass around 1.5–1.7 GeV), but simulations are not proofs.

10. What the Lean check does and doesn't show

Lean is a proof assistant: every logical step is checked mechanically against a small trusted kernel. A successful Lean check establishes

$$\vdash\;\; \texttt{FormalStatement}$$

with essentially no possibility of a hidden logical gap. What it can't establish is that FormalStatement means what humans mean by "quantum Yang–Mills theory exists." The definitions of the measure space, the gauge-invariant observables, the OS axioms and "non-trivial" all had to be written in Lean, and a subtly weak definition could make the theorem easier than the real problem. Reviewing those definitions was the committee's main job.

Caveats on the review
  • OpenAI chose the chair (Gross), who chose the other four members.
  • Reviewers disclosed using OpenAI's own model to help digest the Lean code, and said they do not yet fully understand the proof.
  • The Lean formalisation is public; the long natural-language write-up was reported as not yet released. Independent human understanding and journal-style refereeing are still to come.
