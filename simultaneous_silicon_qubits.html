Running several silicon qubits at once, without losing accuracy

A walk through the RIKEN result (Wu, Camenzind et al., Nature Physics 2026): what goes wrong when qubits share a microwave line, the math of why, and how pairwise calibration fixes most of it. Equations below are a simplified textbook model that captures the mechanism; the paper's exact formulas and parameters may differ.

1. A spin qubit and its gate

A silicon spin qubit is one electron spin in a two-level system, |0⟩ and |1⟩, split by energy ħωq. Its state is a point on the Bloch sphere:

|ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + eiφ sin(θ/2)|1⟩θ sets the population in |1⟩ (latitude); φ is the phase (longitude, rotation about z).

A microwave tone at frequency ωd with amplitude Ω (the Rabi frequency) drives the qubit:

H(t) = (ħωq/2) σz + ħΩ cos(ωdt + φd) σx

In the frame rotating at ωd, dropping fast terms (rotating-wave approximation), with detuning Δ = ωq − ωd:

Hrot = (ħΔ/2) σz + (ħΩ/2)(cos φd σx + sin φd σy)

On resonance (Δ = 0) the state rotates about an axis in the xy-plane at rate Ω. A π pulse (a NOT gate) lasts tπ = π/Ω. Off resonance, the rotation axis tilts toward z and the rate becomes the generalized Rabi frequency:

Ωeff = √(Ω² + Δ²)

2. The shared control line

Giving each qubit its own wire does not scale. Instead, one microwave line addresses many qubits. Each qubit has a slightly different frequency, so the tone at fk is resonant with qubit k and, in principle, ignored by the others (frequency-division addressing).

3. Where the error comes from: the AC Stark shift

When qubit j feels an off-resonant tone from qubit k's drive, with amplitude Ωjk and detuning Δjk = ωj − ωk, second-order perturbation theory in the rotating frame gives an effective Hamiltonian for qubit j:

Heff ≈ (ħδjk/2) σz, δjk ≈ Ωjk² / (2Δjk)Sign follows Δ. The tone does not flip qubit j (that needs resonance), but it shifts its energy gap: ħωj → ħ(ωj + δ). This is the AC Stark shift.

A shift δ acting for the gate duration tg rotates qubit j about z by:

εj = δj tg, δj = Σk ≠ j, k active δjkTo leading order the shifts from different active tones add. More simultaneous drives means a larger total shift. This is why the problem grows with the number of qubits operated together.

4. The fix: pairwise calibration

Because shifts add (section 3), you do not need to measure every combination of active qubits. Measure how much qubit j's frequency moves when only qubit k is driven, for each ordered pair:

sjk = δjk (measured with only drive k on)

Then, whichever set A of qubits is active in a given step, correct each active qubit's phase by the predicted accumulated shift. In practice this is a "virtual Z" update, shifting the phase reference of later pulses by no real hardware action:

φjcorr = − tg Σk ∈ A, k ≠ j sjk

6. What is left: drive-induced decoherence

Stark phase shifts are coherent: a known rotation, so it can be undone. Decoherence is not. Strong microwave power heats the device and couples the spin to noise, adding a dephasing rate that grows with power. A simple model:

Γdrive ≈ κ Ωtot², Ωtot² ≈ Σk Ωk²κ is a device-dependent constant. Driving N qubits at once needs roughly N times the total power.
1 − Fdec ~ Γdrive tg ∝ Ωtot² · (π/Ω)

Calibration cannot undo this, since information has already leaked out, which matches the paper's finding that the remaining loss at five qubits is linked to this effect. The authors suggest device design improvements (for example, less power needed per gate) as the route to reduce it. Note the trade-off visible in the formula: faster gates (larger Ω) shorten tg but raise power.

Caveats: these results are for single-qubit gates on five qubits. Two-qubit gates, far larger arrays, and the assumption that shifts add linearly are separate questions the community still has to settle. Still, it supports the case that shared control lines can scale silicon spin processors made with standard chip fabrication.
